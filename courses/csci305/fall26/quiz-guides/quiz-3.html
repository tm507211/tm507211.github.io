Quiz 3 Study Guide

CSCI 305: Concepts of Programming Languages

This guide lists what you should be able to do, not the questions you will be asked. It ranges a little wider than the quiz itself, and covers Lectures 7–9 and their textbook chapters: program semantics, environments and let, and lexical scope. Answers are short: a sentence or two, in your own words, earns full credit.

Program semantics Lecture 7

Reading: Program semantics  |  Slides: Lecture 7

Be able to:

  • Say what a rule set specifies that an interpreter cannot: an account of what an expression means (e ⇩ v) that stands ahead of any one program, so that calling the evaluator correct has something to be correct against.
  • Read the judgment e ⇩ v and explain what syntax-directed (the outermost form of e selects the rule) and compositional (a compound's value is built from its subexpressions' values) mean.
  • Explain what makes an expression stuck: no derivation concludes e ⇩ v for any v — the absence of a value, not a special value standing for an error. Give the premise that fails for 1 + true (operands must be integers) and for 1 / 0 (the divisor must be nonzero).
  • Explain how the interpreter makes that absence observable — it reports on its error channel (carried up by ?) rather than returning a value — and why the stuck expressions are exactly the ones with no derivation.
  • Read or sketch a small derivation tree (for instance, false and (1 / 0) ⇩ false) and say why the right operand is never evaluated.
  • Explain determinism: e ⇩ v is a partial function — an expression has one value or none, never a choice of two — and why (rules are syntax-directed, and where a form has two rules their premises are mutually exclusive).
  • Name the family of semantic styles — small-step, denotational, axiomatic — and say in a sentence what each is a natural tool for.
  • Contrast big-step with small-step: say what small-step makes visible that big-step does not, and why big-step cannot tell a computation that runs forever from one that is stuck (each simply lacks a finite derivation).

Environments and let Lecture 8

Reading: Environments and let  |  Slides: Lecture 8

Be able to:

  • Explain why a variable's value depends on context — the environment ρ, the map of names in scope to values — while 2 + 2 is 4 anywhere.
  • Read E-Var (ρ(x) = v) and say why a lookup that misses is stuck: it is the one new failure this part adds (UnboundVariable).
  • Read E-Let and state its crucial timing: the right-hand side is evaluated in the environment before the new binding is added, so a name is not in scope in its own right-hand side.
  • Give a block's value: earlier expressions' values are discarded and the block's value is its last expression; an empty block, or a block ending in a let, is ().
  • Explain why a binding cannot leave its block: a block yields a value, not an environment, and extending the environment returns a new scope, leaving the caller's untouched.
  • Explain shadowing with a read-only environment: a second let for a name is a new binding that hides the old one; the earlier value is unreachable, not rewritten, and a lookup reads the newest binding.
  • Recognize the design space: let x = e; rest is the same binding move as (λx. rest)(e); Bridger has no let rec (recursion is a top-level fn) and allows a name to be rebound in the same scope.

Lexical scope Lecture 9

Reading: Lexical scope  |  Slides: Lecture 9

Be able to:

  • Distinguish a free from a bound occurrence, relative to the region you are reading, and say why a binder reaches inward into the blocks it encloses but not to a sibling.
  • State the scope of a binding: from its let to the closing brace of its block (which is exactly what E-Let already fixed).
  • Trace a nesting by hand: the nearest enclosing binder wins, shadowing carves a hole in an outer binding's scope, sibling blocks do not share, and a name free at the top of a program is UnboundVariable.
  • Explain why lexical means static: the binding each occurrence refers to is a function of the program text alone, settleable before the program runs — which is what tools such as go-to-definition and unused-name warnings stand on.
  • Say why the runtime environment chain has the shape of the text, so that looking a name up (walking parent scopes) and reading outward through the braces cannot disagree.
  • Explain that lexical scope is not a feature added to the evaluator — it is a property of E-Let extending the environment over the textual rest of a block.
  • Contrast lexical and dynamic scope: a free name resolves at the definition site under lexical scope and at the call site under dynamic scope. Say why the two agree on every block-only program (code runs where it is written), and why a function — defined in one place, called in another — is what finally makes them diverge. Bridger is lexical.
How to practice
The single best rehearsal is the Concept Checks at the end of each textbook chapter — they are the same kind of short, conceptual question, with worked answers you can check yourself against. Work them with the answers hidden first. Beyond that: take a nested block with a name bound at several depths and, by hand, write the value each occurrence reads and draw each binding's scope (and its holes); read each inference rule and say, in words, what its premises and conclusion require; and work the lexical-vs-dynamic example both ways, saying where each discipline resolves the free name.
