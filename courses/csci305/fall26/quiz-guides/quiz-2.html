Quiz 2 Study Guide

CSCI 305: Concepts of Programming Languages

This guide lists what you should be able to do, not the questions you will be asked. It ranges a little wider than the quiz itself, and covers Lectures 5–6 and their textbook chapters, plus one design question about the coin purse you built for Milestone M0. Answers are short: a sentence or two, in your own words, earns full credit.

What to expect
When
At the start of class, on the date shown in the schedule.
Length
About 15 minutes.
Rules
Closed book, handwritten.
Format
A handful of short conceptual questions, one question that asks you to read a small Rust snippet and say what it prints, and one question about a design decision you made in Milestone M0.
Answers
Explanations in your own words — no memorized dates or trivia, and no need to reproduce exact Rust syntax.

Tree-walking evaluation Lecture 5

Reading: Tree-walking evaluation  |  Slides: Lecture 5

Be able to:

  • Explain why evaluation is a recursive function over the tree: the rules are compositional, so a node's value is computed from its children's values, and a literal is the base case.
  • Read an inference rule and say what each part becomes in the evaluator: a premise becomes a recursive call on a child, the conclusion becomes the value the match arm returns, and one rule is one arm.
  • Say why the evaluator's call tree has the same shape as the rule's derivation tree, and why children must be evaluated before their parent.
  • Explain how the rule for division differs from the rule for addition (the extra premise that the divisor is nonzero), and what the evaluator does when that premise fails.
  • Explain how and and or short-circuit: why each needs two rules, when the right operand is evaluated, and what an expression like false and (1 / 0) evaluates to and why.
  • Name an alternative to walking the tree (a bytecode VM, closure generation, a JIT) and say in a sentence what it trades for what.

Error handling Lecture 6

Reading: Error handling  |  Slides: Lecture 6

Be able to:

  • Distinguish a failure a program expects and can act on (a missing key, a bad parse, a zero divisor) from a stuck state the interpreter halts on, and say why each is handled differently.
  • Say what is wrong with reporting failure through a sentinel value such as -1 or a null pointer.
  • Describe the three categories of mechanism — exceptions, error values, and abort — and for each say where the failure goes, whether the caller is forced to handle it, and whether it is visible in the type.
  • Explain what an exception handler does, and the difference between termination and resumption once a handler runs.
  • Explain rightward drift: why a sequence of fallible operations handled with nested match steps to the right, and how an early return flattens it.
  • Say what the ? operator abbreviates, and trace a small Rust function that uses it to say what it returns for a given input.
  • Say which mechanism Bridger uses for recoverable failure, which it uses for unrecoverable failure, and which it leaves out.
  • Name how Java, Go, and Rust each divide the space, in a sentence apiece.

Milestone M0: your representation choice The coin purse

Reading: Milestone M0: the Rust warm-up

One question asks about a decision you made, not about Rust. You chose how a Purse is represented: a list of every coin, a map from each kind to a count, or one count per kind. Be able to:

  • Say which representation you chose.
  • Name one purse operation your choice made easy and one it made harder than another representation would have, and explain why in terms of what the operation has to do (push one item, scan every coin, merge counts, handle a kind that is absent).
  • Connect this to the same decision the interpreter makes for its own values: a representation makes some operations one step and others a loop.

You will not be asked to recall the exact fields, types, or method signatures you wrote.

How to practice
The single best rehearsal is the Concept Checks at the end of each textbook chapter — they are the same kind of short, conceptual question, with worked answers you can check yourself against. Work them with the answers hidden first. Re-reading the Lecture 5–6 slides and re-typing the small Rust snippets (the miniature evaluator, and the three versions of read_config) is the next most useful thing. For the M0 question, look back at your Purse and ask which methods were one line and which needed a loop.
