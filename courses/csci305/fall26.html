Catalog Description

An examination of the basic concepts of programming languages and several programming paradigms. Concepts will include some of: abstraction mechanisms, static and dynamic typing, scope, syntax vs. semantics, closures, and algebraic datatypes. Paradigms examined may include functional, imperative, logic, and object-oriented.

Course Outcomes

  1. Interpret and develop specifications for various programming languages.
  2. Explain the principles behind the design of programming languages.
  3. Explain different paradigms (e.g., procedural, functional, object-oriented, logical) for programming and for associated languages.
  4. Explain the syntax and semantics of programming languages.
  5. Explain programming language features (e.g., expressions, assignment statements, control statements, data abstraction, variable bindings and scope, object-orientation constructs, exception handling) and their implementations.
  6. Write programs in different programming languages.

Course Project Policies

Submission and Grading

Project milestones are submitted through Gradescope, linked from the course Canvas page. Each milestone is graded automatically against a test suite. Some tests are visible, so you will get results back shortly after submitting and can resubmit before the deadline. Other tests are hidden. Passing every visible test does not guarantee full credit. Some components of each milestone are also reviewed by hand, including the write-up described below.

Write-ups and Explaining Your Work

Every milestone includes a short write-up explaining how your implementation works and why you made the design choices you made. You are responsible for understanding everything you submit, and you may be asked to explain your submission on a quiz or in person. The write-up and any such explanation are graded as part of the milestone.

Generative AI

You are welcome to use generative AI on the course project. I suggest treating it as a TA who is available at any hour: ask it to explain a concept, walk you through an error, or review an approach you are considering. An AGENTS.md / CLAUDE.md file is provided with the project that configures an assistant for this course. You are free to use it, change it, ignore it, or use a different tool entirely.

I will not try to determine how much of a submission you wrote yourself. What comes with that freedom is the responsibility above: you need to be able to explain how your code works and why it is built the way it is. Code you cannot explain will not earn full credit.

This applies to the capstone challenge as well, where you are also responsible for explaining why your solution performs as well as it does. Individual assignment descriptions may refine these policies.

Collaboration

You are encouraged to discuss the project with other students in the course — how a concept works, what a specification means, why an approach is or is not working. Do not share your code with another student, and do not copy another student's code.

Late Work

You have four late days for the semester. A late day cancels one day of lateness on a project milestone. You do not need to request them or tell me when you are using them: at the end of the semester they are applied to whichever milestones benefit your grade the most.

After your late days are applied, each remaining day late costs 10% of that milestone's grade, up to five days. Any part of a day counts as a full day. Submissions are not accepted more than five days after the deadline, and late days do not extend that cutoff; a milestone not turned in by then receives no credit.

If something outside your control gets in the way (e.g., illness, a family emergency, or a similar circumstance) contact me as early as you can. This policy is the default, and I can make exceptions to it far more easily before a deadline than after one.
